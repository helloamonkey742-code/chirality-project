# How many molecules must decide together? Parity-violation selection of homochirality tested in Solar System ocean worlds

**Saptarshi Ghosh**
Cupertino High School, [ADD FULL POSTAL ADDRESS], Cupertino, California, USA

**Corresponding author:** Saptarshi Ghosh, [ADD POSTAL ADDRESS], [ADD TELEPHONE], [ADD E-MAIL]. ORCID: [ADD IF AVAILABLE]

## Abstract

Life uses left-handed amino acids and right-handed sugars. Kondepudi and Nelson proposed that the weak nuclear force, which makes mirror-image molecules differ very slightly in energy (the parity-violating energy difference, PVED), could select life's hand in a large, slow, self-amplifying reaction. I ask when this tiny bias beats random chance. I use a stochastic normal form of Frank-type self-amplification, checked against brute-force simulations and a full reaction network. About 10³³–10³⁴ molecules must decide together. That needs kilometre-scale volumes of well-mixed water. Ponds and rock pores are many orders of magnitude too small, so I use Solar System ocean worlds as test beds. Varying every uncertain input at once, the PVED-favoured hand wins in 22% (Enceladus), 29% (Europa) and 75% (early Earth) of plausible cases. Vertical mixing is the main unknown. Weak mixing freezes the ocean into stacked layers of opposite hand, and a stratified layer under a thin ice shell traps the boundary between hands. Across fourteen worlds, Ganymede (28%) and Europa (22–26%) score highest among confirmed oceans, and Enceladus lowest (0–18%). In a closed reversible network, an amplified excess rises and then fades, while a driven network holds it. This reconciles the model with the racemic amino acids returned from asteroid Bennu. It predicts a lasting excess in a driven ocean such as that of Enceladus. No measured prebiotic reaction yet amplifies handedness this way, so I outline a planned wet-lab search. All code is public.

## 1 Introduction

Life uses left-handed (L) amino acids in proteins and right-handed (D) sugars in DNA and RNA. Each mirror-image form is chemically identical to the other when alone. Why life chose one hand, and why this one, is still open.

Frank (1953) showed that a simple network can amplify a small excess of one hand into a one-handed outcome. The network needs two steps: each hand copies itself from an achiral feedstock, and the two hands destroy each other. The network is symmetric. On its own, it picks a hand by chance.

Kondepudi and Nelson (1985) proposed a way to break that tie. The weak nuclear force violates parity, so mirror-image molecules differ very slightly in energy. This is the parity-violating energy difference (PVED). It is about 10⁻¹⁷ kT. They proposed that a large, slow, self-amplifying reaction could let this tiny bias select life's hand. The PVED has never been measured directly. Whether it or chance chose life's hand remains open (Quack et al., 2022).

Several studies have tested parts of this idea. Stochastic models show that a small bias selects the hand when it exceeds the noise (Hochberg et al., 2023). In open systems, parity-violation-induced selection can survive temperature fluctuations (Hochberg et al., 2022). Lente (2007) found that the PVED cannot be amplified when self-copying starts from a few chiral molecules. My model instead assumes an open, fed reaction in which the whole population chooses at once. Cowan and Furnstahl (2022) combined the PVED with metal-catalysed self-amplifying synthesis in an early-Earth ocean. Brandenburg and Multamäki (2004) studied how long regions of opposite hand can coexist in space, without a weak-force bias. Lunine et al. (1999) discussed abiotic homochirality on Titan and proposed it as a natural laboratory. I consider liquid-water oceans only.

Here I ask two linked questions. First, how many molecules must react together for the PVED to beat chance? Second, does any real body of water meet that requirement? I use the Solar System's ocean worlds as test beds. They matter for a simple reason. All life on Earth shares one ancestor, so Earth's single hand cannot tell a physical bias from luck. A second, independent ocean could.

Section 2 gives the model and the ocean inputs. Section 3 gives the results: a size rule, how mixing and layering shape the outcome, the effect of a stratified layer under the ice, a ranking of fourteen worlds, and checks against meteorites and lab data. Section 4 lists the limitations. Section 5 outlines a planned experiment.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Normal form near the switch-on point

Let α = (L − D)/(L + D) be the excess of one hand. Near the point where a self-amplifying reaction switches on, any such reaction reduces to the same normal form (Figure 1):

$$d\alpha = \left(\lambda(t)\,\alpha - \alpha^3 + g\right)dt + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,dW, \qquad \lambda = \gamma t . \tag{1}$$

- λ measures how far the system is past the switch-on point. Before it (λ < 0), the only stable state is racemic. After it (λ > 0), there are two stable states, all-L and all-D.
- g = ΔE_pv/kT is the weak-force bias, where ΔE_pv is the PVED per molecule. Its sign for real amino acids is unsettled.
- ε ≈ 1/N is the noise from counting individual molecules, where N is the number of molecules that react together.
- γ = 1/(kτ) sets how quickly conditions sweep through the switch-on point. Here k = k₂c is the self-copying rate (rate constant k₂ times concentration c) and τ is the time the sweep takes. Time is in units of 1/k.

Linearising around λ = 0 gives the probability that the PVED-favoured hand wins:

$$P = \Phi(\Delta), \qquad \Delta = \sqrt{2}\,\pi^{1/4}\, g\, \gamma^{-1/4}\, \varepsilon^{-1/2} = \sqrt{2}\,\pi^{1/4}\, g\,(k\tau)^{1/4}\sqrt{N}, \tag{2}$$

where Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution. Δ = 2 means a 97.7% win. I checked Eq. 2 against 4,000 brute-force stochastic runs per point. They agreed within ±0.005.

### 2.2 A full Frank network

I checked the normal form against a full Frank-type network in a flow reactor (Frank, 1953). A is the achiral feedstock (for example an amino-acid precursor). L and D are the two hands of the product.

| Reaction | Rate | Role |
|---|---|---|
| feed → A | f(a₀ − a), a₀ ramped slowly upward | supply; the ramp sweeps the system through switch-on |
| A → L, A → D | k₀a each | slow, unbiased uncatalysed production |
| A + L → 2L | k(1 + g)aL | self-copying; the weak force enters as ±g |
| A + D → 2D | k(1 − g)aD | self-copying |
| L + D → waste | k_i LD | mutual destruction (cross-inhibition) |
| L, D → out | k_d L, k_d D | outflow |

I simulated every reaction with its own counting noise, using the chemical Langevin equation. At the switch-on point the network maps onto Eq. 1 with g_eff = 2gxka, ε = (2k₀a + 2x(ka + k_d))/Ω and γ = k·da/dt. Here x is the concentration of each hand and Ω is the reactor size. Reactions that add or remove a chiral molecule without copying add extra noise. This lowers Δ by a factor F ≈ 0.7 compared with Eq. 2. A second scheme, with reverse reactions, gives F = 0.71 and matches the formula within 1.7 points.

### 2.3 From a reaction to a body of water

For a body of water, N = c·V·f·1000·N_A. Here V is the volume (m³), f is the fraction that is well mixed on the decision time, and N_A is Avogadro's number. The PVED-favoured hand "wins" only if two conditions both hold:

- the size condition Δ ≥ 2;
- the time condition k₂cτ ≥ 10, so that the reaction actually finishes.

### 2.4 Mixing, patches and layers

With mixing, α becomes a field α(x, t):

$$\partial_t \alpha = \lambda\alpha - \alpha^3 + g + D_h\nabla_h^2\alpha + D_z\,\partial_z^2\alpha + \text{noise}. \tag{3}$$

D_h and D_z are the horizontal and vertical eddy diffusivities. Stretching the depth by √(D_h/D_z) turns Eq. 3 exactly into the equal-mixing equation in a box that is taller by that factor. So weak vertical mixing acts like a deep, narrow ocean.

After the decision, walls between regions of opposite hand move at D times their curvature (Allen and Cahn, 1979). Closed patches shrink, patch size grows as l = A√(Dt), and the majority hand takes over. Flat walls between horizontal layers do not move in a flat box. On a sphere, a layer wall of constant depth feels only vertical mixing, and it sinks at 2·D_z/R, where R is the radius.

All spatial runs use explicit Euler–Maruyama time steps with second-order centred differences (the 7-point Laplacian). Walls are periodic sideways, and the top and bottom are closed. Units are 1/k for time and √(D/k) for length, with grid spacing 1 and time step 0.05. This meets the 3D stability limit D·dt/dx² ≤ 1/6.

### 2.6 Test for a stratified lid

Below a salinity S\*, water at its freezing point becomes lighter as it cools. The ocean then stacks a stratified upper layer on a convecting interior (Zeng and Jansen, 2021, 2024). I compute S\* from the TEOS-10 equation of state (McDougall and Barker, 2011) at the pressure at the top of the ocean, 920 kg/m³ × gravity × ice thickness. Under more than about 28 MPa of ice, water at its freezing point sinks as it cools at any salinity, so no lid can form. Worlds with no measured salinity get a log-uniform prior of 1–100 g/kg. A draw counts as pinned, and loses, when its salinity is below S\*. I report these results beside the lid-free numbers, not in place of them.

To test whether such a lid traps a wall, I ran a radial gradient-flow model and axisymmetric hemisphere runs. In these runs the lid cuts vertical mixing only. Separate runs added a polar gap in the lid, or a single overturning current. I wrote a prediction before every run and kept the wrong ones.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 How many molecules must decide together?

Setting Δ = 2 in Eq. 2 gives the number of molecules that must react as one well-mixed pool:

$$N \ge \left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi^{1/4} g\,(k\tau)^{1/4}}\right)^2 . \tag{4}$$

For g = 10⁻¹⁷ (the middle of the modern PVED range) and a reaction that only just finishes (kτ = 10), N ≥ 3.6×10³³ molecules, or 6×10⁹ mol. At 1 µM that is a well-mixed cube of water 18 km on a side; at 1 mM, 1.8 km; at 0.1 M, 0.4 km. A 1 m³ pond at 1 M holds only 6×10²⁶ molecules, about 7 orders of magnitude too few.

The PVED is a fixed energy, so g = ΔE/kT is larger in the cold. The needed N scales as T²: 3.6×10³³ at 0 °C, 4.3×10³³ at 27 °C and 6.7×10³³ at 100 °C. Cold helps only by a factor of about 2. Time helps only weakly, as (kτ)^¼: a sweep 10,000× slower lowers N by 100×. Concentration alone cannot rescue a small setting. Only ocean-scale water that is mixed within the decision time can pool enough molecules (Figure 2).

The same rule applies to connected compartments, such as the pores of a hydrothermal mound. I simulated 16 compartments, each with 1/16 of the molecules, exchanging material at rate q (normal form, g = 10⁻³, γ = 0.01). The chance that the majority took the favoured hand was 0.675 when they were isolated, 0.686 at q × decision time = 0.1, 0.724 at 1 and 0.714 at 10. A single pool holding all the molecules gives 0.724, and one isolated compartment gives 0.559. Compartments that exchange faster than the decision time act as one pool. In a vent mound (k₂ = 10⁻³ M⁻¹ s⁻¹, c = 1 mM, 10⁵ yr), the decision takes about 56 years. In that time diffusion links only about a 2 m³ pond, which gives P = 0.500, a coin flip. If fluid flow connects a 100 m mound (10⁶ m³), P = 0.73. A flow-connected cubic kilometre gives P = 1.000. Compartments help only in proportion to the volume that flow links within the decision time.

### 3.2 An ocean decides in patches, then in layers

Real oceans mix slowly, so the decision happens in many patches at once. In 3D, curved walls straighten, small patches shrink, and patch size grows as l = A√(Dt). I measured exponents of 0.45 and 0.49 (theory 0.5) and A = 5.48 and 5.60 at two mixing strengths. From a 55% majority, a cube heals to one hand (Figure 3, top).

Weak vertical mixing changes this. After stretching, the ocean is deeper than it is wide for 92–96% of the plausible range. In such tall boxes the patchwork froze into 3–5 stacked layers in 7 of 8 runs, unchanged from t = 500 to 1000. A cube healed to one hand in 4 of 4 runs from a 55% start. Figure 3 (bottom) shows layers that do not change from t = 400 to t = 5000.

On a sphere, the layers must merge by sinking. Measured sink speeds were 0.991, 0.991 and 0.984 times 2·D_z/r, and they did not change when D_h was quadrupled. At D_z = 10⁻¹⁰ m² s⁻¹, takeover would take up to 1.5×10¹² yr (Enceladus) or 2.8×10¹³ yr (Europa). The merge-in-time condition holds in 33% (Enceladus) and 34% (Europa) of draws. Vertical mixing is therefore the key unknown for icy moons.

### 3.3 Win rates for Enceladus, Europa and early Earth

Varying every input of Table 1 at once, the PVED-favoured hand wins in 22% of plausible cases for Enceladus, 29% for Europa and 75% for early Earth's open ocean. If layers never merged, the moons would fall to 3% and 7%.

Vertical mixing is the most decisive input. The win rate is 0%, 0% and 52% (Enceladus, Europa, Earth) in the low half of its range, against 43%, 57% and 97% in the high half. Concentration comes next (+21 and +10 points for the moons), then time (+15 and +11; +32 for Earth), then the bias g (+8 and +4) and the rate k₂ (+5). Faster sideways mixing lowers the rate slightly (−2 to −9 points). F and volume each move the moons by 2 points or less.

**What would the unknowns have to be?** With every other input still drawn over its range, a moon passes 50% only through vertical mixing: D_z ≥ 2.7×10⁻⁵ m² s⁻¹ for Enceladus and ≥ 6.1×10⁻⁶ m² s⁻¹ for Europa. No slice of any other input's range takes either moon above 38%. Even in the fastest tenth of vertical mixing the moons level off at 66% and 86%. Early Earth stays above 50% unless D_z < 2.9×10⁻⁶ m² s⁻¹ or the time is under 1.4×10⁷ yr. It wins 87% near the measured thermocline value of 1.1×10⁻⁵ m² s⁻¹ (Ledwell et al., 1993).

**Assumed rules and the bias.** Two rules were assumed rather than measured: the deciding share of the top layer, and the bar Δ ≥ 2. Changing them (scaling the share by 0.1, 0.28 or 2.1, letting the whole ocean decide, or moving the bar to Δ ≥ 1 or Δ ≥ 3) changes a moon by at most 2.2 points and never changes the order. The bias range used (≈0.01–10 aeV) lies at or below the low end of current calculations (Quack et al., 2022). A bias 10× or 100× larger raises Enceladus to 25.7% and 27.8% and Europa to 30.7% and 31.2%. Even an unlimited bias stops at 28.6% and 31.2%, because the reaction must still finish and the layers must still merge in time. Earth is 74.7% in every case.

**Slowly swinging conditions.** Tides, orbital cycles and climate make the drive swing while it ramps. A swing only weakens the effective bias. Mild, plausible and harsh swings give 21%/28%/75%, 20%/26%/75% and 17%/24%/75% (Enceladus/Europa/Earth). Swinging costs the moons at most 5 points and leaves Earth unchanged.

### 3.4 A stratified lid traps the wall between hands

In the radial model, a wall can pass a change in mixing only if |d ln σ/dr| < 2/r, where σ is the wall's energy per area. With the slow layer at the bottom, or with no slow layer, the wall sinks to the floor and one hand takes over. With the slow layer on top or in the middle, the wall stops just above the layer's lower edge and never moves again. Pinning starts at a mixing ratio between 1.02 and 1.05, so any real stratification is far past it. A bias can push the wall through, but it needs h ≈ 0.20 in model units. The weak-force bias is g ≤ 4×10⁻¹⁶, about 15 orders of magnitude too weak.

S\* is 22.5 g/kg at the top of the Enceladus ocean (18.0 at its floor) and 3.0 g/kg under 20 km of ice on Europa (19.5 under 4 km). The measured Enceladus salinity, 5–20 g/kg (Postberg et al., 2009), lies below S\*. Even its poles are stratified under a steady ice shell (Ames et al., 2025). Counting stratified-top draws as losses:

- Enceladus drops from 22% to 0% (stratified in 100% of draws), and stays at 0–8% across the choices tested.
- Europa drops from 29% to 22% (stratified in 24% of draws), and ranges over 10–29% across the choices tested.

**Holes and currents.** A gap in the lid frees the wall only if it is wider than tan θ_c = (h/R)·√(D_side/D_lid). I measured θ_c between 25° and 30° for a 10× slower lid and between 55° and 70° for a 100× slower one. Real polar gaps are far narrower than this. A current crossing the lid can free the wall. For lids 10× and 30× slower, the wall is freed at a Péclet number Pe ≳ 1, where Pe compares the current across the lid with mixing across it. A lid 100× slower stayed pinned up to Pe 80, the strongest current the grid resolves. Early Earth's thermocline gives Pe ~5–10 (Ledwell et al., 1993; Munk, 1966). In a closed-lid run with a fast-mixed surface layer, that is not enough. But Earth's thermocline reaches the surface near the poles, where deep water sinks. With a 20° polar opening and a current sinking at the pole, the wall was freed at Pe 1.2. Earth's 75% therefore holds because its overturning sinks where its thermocline opens. The Enceladus lid sits over a convecting interior with a mixing contrast of at least 1000×, so its result stands.

### 3.6 Closed rocks fade; driven oceans hold

Samples returned from asteroid Bennu, plausibly a fragment of a wet parent body, carry racemic or nearly racemic chiral amino acids (Glavin et al., 2025). This tests the model, because the model assumes that some amplifying chemistry exists. With assumed but literature-bracketed inputs (Glavin et al., 2025; Glavin and Dworkin, 2009), c·τ on Bennu's parent body was 3×10⁸ to 9×10¹¹ M·s. Every draw in the original k₂ range would have finished there. If an amplified excess lasted forever, Bennu would rule out that whole range. With k₂ extended down to 10⁻¹² M⁻¹ s⁻¹, the PVED would win in only 2% (Enceladus), 4% (Europa) and 15% (early Earth) of the draws that keep Bennu racemic.

But amplification is a non-equilibrium process. At equilibrium a closed system must end racemic. In a closed, reversible Frank network the excess can rise for a while and then fall back (Blanco et al., 2011; Stich et al., 2016), and a recent prebiotic network behaves the same way (Higgs and Blackmond, 2025). A lasting excess needs a constant supply of free energy (Blackmond, 2004; Plasson et al., 2007). I reproduced this with my own network. I made its production steps reversible, with rates that obey detailed balance, and ran it closed from a 0.1% seed:

- With each reverse rate half its forward rate (s = 1), the excess peaks at 70% and falls below a tenth of its peak after about 7×10³ reaction times.
- With reverse steps 100× slower (s = 0.01), it peaks at 100% and fades after about 2×10⁶.
- The same network kept open (fed and flushed) holds 99.8% for as long as it is driven.

Bennu's parent body was closed, and its water lasted only while short-lived radioactive heat did. If the excess rose and then faded, Bennu stays racemic without ruling out the amplifier. Counting this, the PVED wins (Enceladus/Europa/Earth):

- 22%/29%/75% (s = 1) and 23%/30%/75% (s = 0.01) in the original k₂ range;
- 15%/21%/57% and 17%/22%/55% in the extended range.

Reading the excess off the full closed-network curve at the moment the water left, instead of using this step rule, agrees within 2 points. That method also shows that a racemic Bennu is the likely outcome mainly when reverse steps are fast: 97% and 73% of Bennu-like draws freeze racemic with s = 1, against 64% and 43% with s = 0.01. Measured reverse rates would therefore decide how much Bennu counts against the model.

This relies on two assumptions. First, an icy-moon ocean must be driven continuously. Molecular hydrogen in the Enceladus plume points to ongoing water–rock reactions (Waite et al., 2017), and tidal heating can keep hydrothermal activity going for tens of Myr to Gyr (Choblet et al., 2017). Second, the chemical drive on Bennu's parent body must have stopped while liquid water remained. If the drive and the water ended together, any excess would have frozen in, and the conflict returns. I therefore report both the 22%/29%/75% and the 2%/4%/15% results as possible. One point stays open: Bennu's isovaline cannot racemize, so the fade rule does not explain why it is racemic.

**A testable prediction.** Without an amplifier, the Enceladus ocean is expected to be racemic, because racemization outpaces production (Steel et al., 2017). A lasting enantiomeric excess in plume amino acids would therefore be a clean sign that something amplifies it. A racemic plume would count against the idea. Capillary-electrophoresis instruments built for ocean worlds can separate enantiomers in salty samples (Mora et al., 2022). Europa Clipper's Surface Dust Analyser (SUDA), a time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Kempf et al., 2025), cannot make this measurement, because mirror-image molecules have the same mass.

### 3.7 Checks against published laboratory amplifiers

I checked the counting rule of Section 3.1 against three real amplifiers. These are reanalyses of published numbers, not pre-registered tests.

- With no added bias, outcomes split as chance predicts: 46 of 91 spontaneous Soai-reaction runs (Singleton and Vo, 2003; Soai et al., 2003; Soai, 2019) and 37 of 73 crystal-deracemization runs (Miyagawa et al., 2019) gave one hand, both consistent with 50/50.
- Of 25 side-by-side identical Soai pairs, 12 gave opposite hands. This rejects a shared bias within each pair strong enough to steer (P = 0.018).
- A 5% crystal excess over more than 10⁶ crystals always won (Viedma, 2005).
- In stirred sodium chlorate crystallization, the whole beaker behaves as about one decision (n_eff ≈ 1.0–1.1) when the stirrer breaks crystals, and about 17 when unstirred (Kondepudi et al., 1990; Martin et al., 1996).

So both a self-copying step and transport are needed, as the model assumes. These checks support the counting rule at lab scale. They do not test the weak force, the 10³³-molecule threshold or any ocean. Lab tests of the Soai reaction also need biases far larger than the PVED to set the outcome (Hawbaker and Blackmond, 2019), as expected for flask-sized volumes.

### 3.8 Stability of the decided hand

A decided pool does not drift. With the reaction held at saturation for 100× longer than the decision, the share of runs on the favoured hand stayed at 0.730 throughout. A pool flips only by a rare noise-driven jump. The measured flip rate matches Kramers' escape rate (Kramers, 1940) within 5%, so it falls as e^(−N/2). A droplet of about 600 molecules can flip; a pond or an ocean never does. Halving the grid spacing changed the coarsening patch sizes by less than 5% at every time.

## 4 Discussion and limitations

The model turns an old qualitative idea into a size rule that any proposed setting can be checked against. About 10³³–10³⁴ molecules must decide together. Ponds, pores and diffusion-linked vent mounds fall short. Oceans clear the size bar, and then the question becomes how they mix. For origin-of-life chemistry this means that a PVED-selected hand needs a large, slow, continuously driven reactor. A flask, a pond or a closed rock will not do.

Earth's one unique push does not rescue the PVED. Magnetite magnetized by a planetary field can give an RNA precursor a large excess (Ozturk et al., 2023a, 2023b). But the field points opposite ways in the two hemispheres, and its polarity at any time is random. In my early-Earth runs it makes the outcome a coin flip (L 35%, D 35%, no single hand 31%) unless fewer than about 2 in 10¹⁵ of the deciding molecules form on magnetized magnetite (below 1.9×10⁻¹⁵). For Europa and Enceladus, the PVED keeps its lead only if less than about 2–4×10⁻¹⁵ of the deciding chemistry occurs on such surfaces.

The main limitations are as follows.

- **All results are order-of-magnitude estimates.** The factor F ≈ 0.7 is checked for only two reaction schemes. Another scheme could give a smaller F.
- **Mixing is the key unknown.** Vertical mixing in icy-moon oceans is a model input, uncertain across 10⁻¹⁰–10⁻³ m² s⁻¹ (Zeng and Jansen, 2021, 2024; Kang et al., 2022). Sideways eddy mixing (Zhang et al., 2024) never limits healing. The deciding top layer's share is an estimate. The mixing model is simple eddy diffusion, with one idealized overturning cell, not a full circulation model.
- **The time τ is set to the ocean's age.** This is the most favourable case.
- **Not modelled:** salt layers deep in large oceans (the "club sandwich" of Vance et al. (2014); if they pinned Ganymede half the time, it would drop to 14%); ammonia, which is likely in outer-Solar-System oceans but missing from TEOS-10; and temperature differences between settings.
- **The bias itself is uncertain.** Its sign for amino acids in water is unsettled, and the sugar PVED may point the "wrong" way (Quack et al., 2022).
- **Bennu** depends on its parent body behaving as a closed system and on unmeasured reverse rates (Section 3.6).
- **No measured prebiotic reaction does this yet.** The Soai reaction amplifies but is not prebiotic. The closest prebiotic candidates are peptide ligations that amplify an excess (Deng et al., 2024) and a network that combines them with amino-acid synthesis and breaks symmetry on its own (Higgs and Blackmond, 2025). Its rates are given as ratios, so k₂ is still a swept assumption. The nearest lab-studied step is a peptide-catalysed transamination that makes enantioenriched alanine (Yu et al., 2024). A rough rate from it lies inside the swept range of k₂.

The testable result is a ranking, not an absolute probability. A deep ocean with no lid (Ganymede) or a thick-shelled Europa is a better place than Enceladus to look for a hand chosen by the weak force.

## 5 Planned experiment

No known prebiotic reaction meets all the needs of the model: self-copying, mutual suppression, prebiotic plausibility and a biologically relevant product. I have therefore pre-registered a wet-lab search for one. It has **not yet been run**. The hypothesis is that at least one prebiotic, water-based, peptide-forming system will show seed-following growth of enantiomeric excess (ee).

The smallest detectable rate is k₂,min = ln(1 + 3√2 σ/e₀)/(cT). Here σ is the ee noise per sample, e₀ the seed excess, c the concentration and T the run length. At realistic concentrations, the rate needed for a pond is 3×10⁻⁶ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ (1 mM, 100 yr), and for an ocean 3×10⁻⁹ (1 µM, 10⁸ yr). No lab run can reach the ocean value at natural concentration. The design therefore has two arms.

1. **Realistic arm:** 1 mM, a 20% seed, 3 years, and 9 independent vials per condition at 2.6% ee error per vial (Glavin and Dworkin, 2009). This reaches 1.8×10⁻⁶ M⁻¹ s⁻¹, below the pond value. With only 3 vials it would reach 2.9×10⁻⁶.
2. **Accelerated arm:** 0.01 M and 0.1 M, stated plainly as not natural. It looks for any amplifier and measures how the rate of ee growth scales with concentration.

Five chemistries are tested:

- (A) amino acids with carbonyl sulfide (Leman et al., 2004);
- (B) amino acids with hydroxy acids under wet–dry cycling (Forsythe et al., 2015);
- (C) cysteine peptides that catalyse their own ligation (Foden et al., 2020);
- (D) an RNA precursor on magnetite (Ozturk et al., 2023b);
- (E) peptide-catalysed transamination coupled to ligation, run fed rather than closed (Yu et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2024; Higgs and Blackmond, 2025).

Arms E and C are the priority. A Viedma grinding positive control (Viedma, 2005) checks that the pipeline can detect real amplification. Each arm gets +L, +D (mirror control) and racemic seeds, plus negative controls (no activator, sterile-filtered, seed alone). Analysts are blinded. An arm counts as an amplifier only if all of the following hold:

- both seeded sets rise by more than the detection threshold;
- the L- and D-seeded sets change by equal and opposite amounts;
- the racemic vials stay at zero;
- the controls stay flat.

The D-seeded mirror arm is the main guard against contamination by biological L-amino acids.

## 6 Conclusions

- For the PVED to beat chance, about 10³³–10³⁴ molecules must decide together. Ponds and rock pores fall short by many orders of magnitude. If the weak force chose life's hand, it did so in an ocean.
- Across plausible inputs, the PVED-favoured hand wins in 75% of early-Earth cases and 22% (Enceladus) and 29% (Europa) before the lid test. Vertical mixing is the main unknown.
- A stratified lid under thin ice traps the wall between hands. Among confirmed oceans, Ganymede (28%) and Europa (22–26%) score highest and Enceladus lowest (0–18%).
- An amplified excess fades in a closed rock and holds in a driven ocean. This reconciles the model with racemic Bennu, with one open point (Bennu's isovaline), and predicts a lasting excess in a driven ocean such as that of Enceladus.
- The missing piece is a prebiotic amplifier. A bench-top search sized for realistic concentrations is planned.
