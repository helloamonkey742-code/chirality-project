### 1.1 Previous proposals that the weak force selected biological handedness

The idea that the weak nuclear force set the hand of life is almost as old as the discovery that the weak force violates parity (1956-1957; Quack et al., 2022). I trace it in order, because my model is a direct descendant of one branch of it. Items marked [CHECK FULL TEXT] go beyond what I could confirm from the title and abstract.

**An amplifier before a bias (1953).** Before parity violation was known, Frank (1953) had already proposed how a chemically symmetric system could end up with one hand. In his scheme, autocatalysis plus mutual inhibition lets small random fluctuations in a racemic mixture tip the system almost entirely into one enantiomer (Frank, 1953, as summarised in the abstract of Soai et al., 1995). The outcome is set by chance. What was missing was a systematic push.

**Parity violation as the push (1959-1968).** Ulbricht (1959) reviewed the new non-conservation of parity together with optical activity. In the same year, Vester, Ulbricht and Krauch (1959) connected optical activity with parity violation in beta-decay. Ulbricht and Vester (1962) then reported attempts to induce optical activity with polarized beta-radiation. The specific mechanism they tested, including the role of circularly polarized bremsstrahlung from the beta-electrons, and the outcome of their experiments are [CHECK FULL TEXT]. Yamagata (1966) put forward a hypothesis for the asymmetric appearance of biomolecules on Earth. Later work names the link between electroweak energy differences and biomolecular homochirality "the Yamagata hypothesis" (Wesendrup et al., 2003). The core of that hypothesis, that a tiny energy bias accumulates through repeated steps or through amplification until one hand dominates, is how later authors summarise it; I have not confirmed this wording against Yamagata's paper [CHECK FULL TEXT]. Garay (1968) bombarded D- and L-tyrosine with beta-particles and found that the D isomer decomposed more quickly.

**Two separate weak-force routes (1974-1982).** From here the field split into two routes. The first is the chiral radiation from beta-decay, which acts at a particular place and time. The second is the parity-violating energy difference (PVED) between enantiomers, which comes from weak neutral currents inside each molecule. Rein (1974) discussed parity-violating effects of intramolecular interactions, and Letokhov (1975) discussed the resulting difference between the energy levels of left- and right-handed molecules; what each proposed beyond this is [CHECK FULL TEXT]. Hegstrom, Rein and Sandars (1980) developed the theory of these energy shifts. Their calculations for twisted ethylene and A-nor-2-thiacholestane gave shifts appreciably smaller than earlier estimates. On the beta-decay route, Hegstrom (1982) found a mechanism by which longitudinally polarized electrons ionize a chiral molecule and its mirror image at different rates. In companion experiments, Gidley et al. (1982) used a spin-polarized positron beam and found no asymmetry in cystine or tryptophan at the 7 x 10^-4 level, but a possible effect of (31 +/- 7) x 10^-4 in leucine.

**Ab initio PVEDs for biomolecules (1983-1985).** Mason and Tranter calculated the PVED between enantiomeric molecules (Mason and Tranter, 1983, 1984). Their ab initio results gave L-alanine in its aqueous conformation, and L-peptides in the alpha-helix and beta-sheet, a lower ground-state energy than the D forms. The difference was of order 10^-14 J mol^-1. That corresponds to an excess of about 10^6 L molecules per mole of racemate at equilibrium at ambient temperature (Mason and Tranter, 1985). Tranter (1985a) extended the calculations to alpha-amino acids. Mason (1987) summarised the position: the weak neutral current gives a constant, uniform chiral bias that lowers the energy of L-amino acids and of the parent aldotriose of the D-sugar series. He set this against classical chiral fields and beta-decay agents, which need a particular time or place on Earth. He also named two ways a molecular-scale bias could matter: as a trigger that guides an autocatalytic racemic process into one channel, or through cumulative amplification in a large homochiral aggregate (Mason, 1987). Tranter (1985b) stated the cost of the first route plainly. The energy differences are so small for simple chiral molecules that propagating homochirality would require an amplification mechanism with both large quantities of reactants and a long reaction time.

**Bias at a bifurcation: Kondepudi and Nelson (1983-1987).** This is the work my model builds on most directly. Kondepudi and Nelson (1983, 1984a) analysed chiral symmetry breaking in nonequilibrium chemical systems and the sensitivity of the symmetry-breaking states, and Kondepudi and Nelson (1984b) derived time scales for chiral selection. In model systems that spontaneously evolve to an L- or D-dominated state, they showed a simple and very sensitive mechanism. Through it, a systematic chiral interaction no stronger than the weak-neutral-current energy in amino acids can determine which enantiomer dominates over about 15,000 years (Kondepudi and Nelson, 1985). They noted that this fits Mason and Tranter's finding for L-amino acids. Kondepudi (1987) gave the general form: slow passage through the transition point makes the system sensitive to very weak but systematic chiral influences. Their treatment of random fluctuations as the competitor to the bias, and their estimate of the reactor volume needed, are [CHECK FULL TEXT].

**Doubts about the size and the sign (1991-2007).** Bonner (1991) reviewed the origin and amplification of biomolecular chirality [CHECK FULL TEXT for its conclusions on the weak force]. Calculations for sugars showed that the lower-energy enantiomer depends on conformation. D-sugars have the lower energy with a C2-endo furanose pucker, and L-sugars with a C3-endo pucker (Tranter et al., 1992). MacDermott (1995) reported PVEDs for a range of important biomolecules, with the natural enantiomer the more stable in nearly all cases. Bonner (1996) concluded that there was no experimental evidence that beta-decay electrons or other spin-polarized particles generate chiral molecules. He also concluded that only theory, not experiment, supported a causal link between PVEDs and homochirality. In his own crystallization tests with very efficient stereoselective autocatalysis, he found no bias. Bonner (2000) went further and concluded that nuclear-level and biopolymer-level parity violation are entirely independent. Improved electroweak quantum chemistry then changed the numbers. Berger and Quack (2000) concluded that numerous earlier claims of L-alanine stabilization were unjustified. Laerdahl, Wesendrup and Schwerdtfeger (2000) found L-alanine lower in energy for only seven of 13 gas-phase conformers, which allowed no definite conclusion. Avalos et al. (2000) revisited the chemical implications of parity violation [CHECK FULL TEXT]. Wesendrup et al. (2003) re-examined the Yamagata hypothesis itself [CHECK FULL TEXT for method and result]. Quack (2002) reviewed the field and noted that the newer methods give PVEDs one to two orders of magnitude larger than previously accepted, but still very small (femtojoule to picojoule per mole). Lente then treated the problem stochastically (Lente, 2004). He found that the chance of the more stable isomer being in excess in a racemic mixture is not significantly above 50% under normal conditions (Lente, 2006). He also found that the PVED cannot be amplified by enantioselective autocatalysis under conditions reasonable for the origin of biological chirality (Lente, 2007). The PVED has still not been detected experimentally. Current calculations put it at about 10^-11 to 10^-10 J mol^-1 (Quack et al., 2022).

**Amplifiers that work in the laboratory (1995-2007).** Meanwhile, real amplifiers were found. Soai et al. (1995) showed that an autocatalytic organozinc reaction raises a 2% enantiomeric excess of a pyrimidyl alkanol. Blackmond (2004) explained this: the product catalyses its own formation and suppresses that of its enantiomer, as in Frank's model. Viedma (2005) showed that grinding-driven crystallization with recycling turns a symmetric population of D and L crystals into one of complete chiral purity. Plasson et al. (2007) classified these far-from-equilibrium mechanisms and stressed recycling and network autocatalysis. These systems supply the amplifier that the Kondepudi-Nelson argument assumes. None of them is the aqueous prebiotic reaction that the argument needs (Section 4).

**Space, planets and noise (1999-2023).** Several studies moved the question beyond a single beaker. Lunine et al. (1999) discussed the abiotic generation of homochirality on Titan; I cite this chapter only for its subject, because I have not read it. Brandenburg and Multamäki (2004) solved reaction-diffusion equations for left- and right-handed populations on Earth's surface. They found that global homochirality is set by the long global diffusion time, which turbulence and large-scale flows can shorten. They also suggested that a quiescent deep ocean could have let opposite hands coexist. Gleiser (2007) used a biased model with cross-inhibition to follow the growth of left- and right-handed domains in space. He bounded the chiral bias from constraints on the emergence of life on early Earth and discussed weak neutral currents and circularly polarized light as sources; he noted that the approach applies to any planetary setting. Gleiser and Walker (2012) argued that environmental disturbances may have set life's hand. They predicted that a representative sample of extraterrestrial stereochemistry will be racemic on average. Hochberg et al. (2022) reported that parity-violation-induced chiral selectivity is resilient to nonequilibrium temperature fluctuations in open systems. Hochberg et al. (2023) framed this as an imperfect bifurcation in which chiral selectivity holds when the bias exceeds the noise.

**Competing deterministic sources.** Two other systematic biases need mention. Circularly polarized infrared light is strong in the Orion OMC-1 star-forming region. Polarized light at shorter wavelengths could bias interstellar organics that were later delivered to Earth (Bailey et al., 1998). Spin-polarized electrons from UV-irradiated magnetite could drive enantioselective reductions through the chiral-induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect (Ozturk and Sasselov, 2022). Crystallizing an RNA precursor on magnetite gave about 60% ee, and 100% after recrystallization (Ozturk et al., 2023b). Enantiopure crystals can in turn magnetize the surface (Ozturk et al., 2023a). Both are local agents of the kind Mason (1987) contrasted with the uniform weak force. Section 4 shows how the magnetite route enters my early-Earth and icy-moon results.

**What this work adds.** I keep the structure of the Kondepudi-Nelson model: a Frank-type amplifier crosses a symmetry-breaking bifurcation, and the PVED acts as a small systematic bias against random fluctuations. Following Hochberg et al. (2023), selection holds only when the bias exceeds the noise. What I add is space. The "large quantities of reactants and a long reaction time" that Tranter (1985b) identified make sense only if all those molecules belong to one connected, well-mixed reacting system. I make that requirement explicit as a count. About 10^33 to 10^34 molecules must take part in one connected, well-mixed volume for the PVED to determine the outcome more often than chance does (Section 3.1). This is consistent with Lente's (2006, 2007) conclusion that the bias is lost in ordinary settings. The scale rule says how large a setting would have to be instead. I then ask whether any real body of water provides such a volume. Ponds and rock pores do not. Ocean worlds such as Enceladus, Europa and Ganymede clear the size bar. Whether they stay connected depends on vertical mixing and stratification, which can break an ocean into patches and stacked layers that evolve separately. Brandenburg and Multamäki (2004) and Gleiser (2007) studied that kind of domain growth on a single planet. The result is a ranking of named worlds, not a general argument. It does not settle the earlier objections: if the PVED is smaller than assumed, or has the opposite sign for the relevant prebiotic molecules (Berger and Quack, 2000; Laerdahl et al., 2000; Tranter et al., 1992), the scale rule and the ranking change with it.
